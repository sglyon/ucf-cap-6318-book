Prerequisites

Outcomes

  • Formulate a platform’s two-sided pricing problem

  • Derive and solve first-order conditions numerically in Julia

  • Explain why optimal platform prices can be zero or negative on one side

  • Distinguish price structure from price level and apply the distinction to real cases

References

  • Required primary text: Easley & Kleinberg, Networks, Crowds, and Markets, chapter 17

  • Required: Rochet & Tirole, Two-Sided Markets: An Overview

  • Hagiu & Wright (2014)

Ladies’ Night¶

  • A nightclub charges men $20 and women $0

  • Unfair—or is the club solving an optimization problem?

  • A person on either side values the club more when the other side shows up

  • Charging everyone the same price may leave the dance floor empty

  • Today we solve the platform’s two-sided problem and derive this asymmetry

The Pattern Is Everywhere¶

  • Credit cards: cardholders receive rewards while merchants pay roughly 2–3%

  • OpenTable: diners search and book for free while restaurants pay per seated diner

  • Google search: users search for free while advertisers fund the system

  • These are not three unrelated discounts

  • They are choices about who pays so that both sides participate

From Ignition to Pricing¶

  • In Platforms and Matchmakers we separated same-side network effects from cross-side network effects

  • In Ignition the chicken-and-egg problem made it hard to reach critical mass

  • Once participation passes a threshold, feedback can create tipping

  • Pricing is one lever for getting the fragile side onto the platform

  • First, however, we need a one-sided benchmark

Build 1: A One-Sided Warm-Up¶

  • Suppose a single firm (a monopolist: no competitors) sells one product to one group of customers

  • Demand falls linearly as price rises

  • The firm trades off a larger margin against fewer sales

  • There is no second group whose participation changes customer value

Solve It by Hand¶

  • Let demand be D(p)=a−bpD(p)=a-bpD(p)=a−bp: at price ppp, the firm sells a−bpa-bpa−bp units

    • aaa is how many units would sell if the product were free

    • bbb is how many sales the firm loses for each $1 increase in price

  • Each unit costs ccc to make (the marginal cost), so each sale earns a margin of p−cp-cp−c

  • Profit is margin times quantity sold:

π(p)=(p−c)(a−bp).\pi(p)=(p-c)(a-bp).π(p)=(p−c)(a−bp).
(1)

  • Here is the tradeoff: raising ppp earns more on every unit sold, but sells bbb fewer units per dollar

  • The best price balances the two forces. At that price, nudging ppp up or down no longer changes profit, so the slope of π\piπ is zero:

π′(p)=a+bc−2bp=0.\pi'(p)=a+bc-2bp=0.π′(p)=a+bc−2bp=0.
(2)

  • Solving for ppp gives the profit-maximizing price:

p∗=a+bc2b=12(ab+c).p^*=\frac{a+bc}{2b}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a}{b}+c\right).p∗=2ba+bc​=21​(ba​+c).
(3)

  • The ratio a/ba/ba/b is the choke price: the price at which demand hits zero, since D(a/b)=a−b⋅ab=0D(a/b)=a-b\cdot\frac{a}{b}=0D(a/b)=a−b⋅ba​=0. Charge that much and nobody buys

  • So p∗p^*p∗ sits exactly halfway between the cost of a unit (ccc) and the price that chokes off all demand (a/ba/ba/b)

Build 2: Two Sides Must Meet¶

  • Now the platform serves buyers BBB and sellers SSS

  • It sets per-interaction prices pBp_BpB​ and pSp_SpS​

  • One buyer without a seller creates no interaction

  • One seller without a buyer creates no interaction

  • This complementarity is the whole model

A Simplified Rochet–Tirole Model¶

  • Prices here are per interaction: a buyer pays pBp_BpB​ only when a transaction happens, and a seller pays pSp_SpS​

  • Willingness to transact on each side responds to its own price:

DB(pB)=aB−bBpB,DS(pS)=aS−bSpS.D_B(p_B)=a_B-b_Bp_B, \qquad D_S(p_S)=a_S-b_Sp_S.DB​(pB​)=aB​−bB​pB​,DS​(pS​)=aS​−bS​pS​.
(4)

  • Wait: shouldn’t buyer demand depend on how many sellers there are?

  • Not here. Joining is free, so a buyer only asks “is this transaction worth pBp_BpB​?”

  • The other side enters through volume: each willing buyer can transact with each willing seller, so volume is DBDSD_BD_SDB​DS​

  • One more willing seller adds DBD_BDB​ transactions. Rochet and Tirole call this a usage externality

  • With cost ccc per interaction, the margin is pB+pS−cp_B+p_S-cpB​+pS​−c

  • Profit is

π(pB,pS)=(pB+pS−c)DB(pB)DS(pS).\pi(p_B,p_S)=(p_B+p_S-c)D_B(p_B)D_S(p_S).π(pB​,pS​)=(pB​+pS​−c)DB​(pB​)DS​(pS​).
(5)

  • We restrict attention to prices with DB≥0D_B\geq 0DB​≥0 and DS≥0D_S\geq 0DS​≥0

  • Later we switch to fees for joining, where the other side’s size does enter demand

The Cross-Side Externality¶

  • Raise pBp_BpB​ and some buyers leave

  • A one-sided firm counts the lost buyer sales

  • A platform also counts the value destroyed for every seller

  • The same logic runs from sellers back to buyers

  • This is a cross-side network effect inside the profit function

  • Question: should the two sides still pay the same price?

  • this figure traces the chain in both directions

Raising pBp_BpB​ loses buyer sales directly; because profit depends on DB⋅DSD_B\cdot D_SDB​⋅DS​, fewer buyers also means every seller completes fewer transactions, even though no seller leaves. The same chain runs in reverse from a seller-price increase back to buyers.

D_B(p_B, pars) = pars.aB - pars.bB * p_B
D_S(p_S, pars) = pars.aS - pars.bS * p_S

function platform_profit(p_B, p_S, pars)
    buyers = D_B(p_B, pars)
    sellers = D_S(p_S, pars)
    if buyers < 0 || sellers < 0
        return -Inf
    end
    return (p_B + p_S - pars.c) * buyers * sellers
end

Derive the First-Order Conditions¶

  • Write total per-interaction margin as m=pB+pS−cm=p_B+p_S-cm=pB​+pS​−c

  • For positive participation, differentiating gives

∂π∂pB=DS[DB−bBm]=0,\frac{\partial \pi}{\partial p_B}=D_S[D_B-b_Bm]=0,∂pB​∂π​=DS​[DB​−bB​m]=0,
(6)
∂π∂pS=DB[DS−bSm]=0.\frac{\partial \pi}{\partial p_S}=D_B[D_S-b_Sm]=0.∂pS​∂π​=DB​[DS​−bS​m]=0.
(7)

  • Substitute linear demand and divide by the slopes:

2pB+pS=aBbB+c,pB+2pS=aSbS+c.2p_B+p_S=\frac{a_B}{b_B}+c, \qquad p_B+2p_S=\frac{a_S}{b_S}+c.2pB​+pS​=bB​aB​​+c,pB​+2pS​=bS​aS​​+c.

  • Solving yields

pB∗=23aBbB−13aSbS+c3,p_B^*=\frac{2}{3}\frac{a_B}{b_B}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{a_S}{b_S}+\frac{c}{3},pB∗​=32​bB​aB​​−31​bS​aS​​+3c​,
(9)

(10)

Detour: Newton’s Method¶

  • In Platforms and Matchmakers we found fixed points with bisection: keep an interval where the function changes sign, then halve it

  • Bisection is safe but slow: each step gains about one binary digit of accuracy. It also only works for one unknown

  • Newton’s method uses the slope instead of a bracket

  • At the current guess xkx_kxk​, replace fff with its tangent line and jump to where that line crosses zero:

xk+1=xk−f(xk)f′(xk).x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}.xk+1​=xk​−f′(xk​)f(xk​)​.
(11)

  • Near a root, the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step

  • The cost: we need the derivative and a reasonable starting guess. Far from a root, or where f′(x)≈0f'(x)\approx 0f′(x)≈0, the jump can overshoot

  • Try it on f(x)=x2−2f(x)=x^2-2f(x)=x2−2, whose root is 2\sqrt{2}2​

root2_gap(x) = x^2 - 2
root2_slope(x) = 2x

function newton_path(f, fprime, x; steps=5)
    path = [x]
    for _ in 1:steps
        x -= f(x) / fprime(x)
        push!(path, x)
    end
    return path
end

From One Equation to Two¶

  • The error column drops from about 10-1 to 10-12 in four steps; bisection would need about 40 halvings

  • Our first-order conditions are two equations in two unknowns: F(x)=0F(x)=0F(x)=0 with x=(pB,pS)x=(p_B,p_S)x=(pB​,pS​)

  • The derivative becomes a matrix of partial derivatives, the Jacobian JJJ, with entries Jij=∂Fi/∂xjJ_{ij}=\partial F_i/\partial x_jJij​=∂Fi​/∂xj​

  • The tangent line becomes a tangent plane, and dividing by f′f'f′ becomes solving a linear system:

xk+1=xk−J(xk)−1F(xk).x_{k+1}=x_k-J(x_k)^{-1}F(x_k).xk+1​=xk​−J(xk​)−1F(xk​).
(12)

  • In Julia, J \ F solves JΔ=FJ\Delta=FJΔ=F without forming the inverse

  • ForwardDiff.jacobian computes JJJ by automatic differentiation, so we don’t derive it by hand

  • newton_system below is exactly this loop, stopping once ∥F(x)∥\lVert F(x)\rVert∥F(x)∥ is tiny

function platform_focs(x, pars)
    p_B, p_S = x
    margin = p_B + p_S - pars.c
    buyers = D_B(p_B, pars)
    sellers = D_S(p_S, pars)
    return [sellers * (buyers - pars.bB * margin),
            buyers * (sellers - pars.bS * margin)]
end

function newton_system(f, x0; tolerance=1e-10, max_iterations=50)
    x = Float64.(x0)
    for iteration in 1:max_iterations
        residual = f(x)
        if norm(residual, Inf) < tolerance
            return (root=x, iterations=iteration - 1, residual=norm(residual, Inf))
        end
        jacobian = ForwardDiff.jacobian(f, x)
        x -= jacobian \ residual
    end
    error("Newton's method did not converge")
end

Subsidize the Sensitive Side¶

  • The optimal buyer price is about −4.33

  • The optimal seller price is about 13.17

  • The platform pays buyers to participate and charges sellers for access to them

  • Yet the total price is about 8.83 and the per-interaction margin is about 6.83

  • Ladies’ Night, cashback rewards, and free restaurant reservations fall out of the same first-order conditions

  • A negative price is not irrational when it creates profitable activity on the other side

Price Structure vs Price Level¶

  • The price level is the total charge per interaction: pB+pSp_B+p_SpB​+pS​

  • The price structure is how that total is divided between the two sides

  • Holding pB+pSp_B+p_SpB​+pS​ fixed but changing the split changes participation and volume

  • That would be impossible in a genuinely one-sided market

  • This sensitivity to price structure is Rochet and Tirole’s key definition of two-sidedness

Reading the Sweep¶

  • The buyer price crosses zero at bB≈1.11b_B\approx 1.11bB​≈1.11 for these parameters

  • Beyond that point, greater buyer sensitivity produces a larger buyer subsidy

  • The seller price rises because sellers benefit from the participation that subsidy creates

  • A one-sided monopolist would NEVER price below cost; a platform does it routinely and rationally

  • The qualification matters: the platform earns revenue from the cross-side response

Fleet Cards Ran This Experiment¶

  • Trucking fleets give drivers fuel cards; truck stops accept them

  • A typical transaction earns the card company $2.00: $0.50 from the fleet, $1.50 from the truck stop (Evans & Schmalensee, Matchmakers, ch. 6)

  • Price level: $2.00. Price structure: 25% fleets, 75% stops

  • It was not always tilted this way

    • Early on, fleets paid and truck stops were subsidized

    • Then a roughly even split

    • Late 1990s: fuel prices and competition squeezed fleets, and they became far more price sensitive

    • Around 2000, prices fell for fleets and rose for stops

  • That is our sweep, played out by a real industry: bBb_BbB​ rose, pB∗p_B^*pB∗​ fell, pS∗p_S^*pS∗​ rose

  • the diagram below tracks the price structure through all three eras

Fleet card pricing began with fleets paying the full fee, drifted to roughly even once truck stops joined in, and after fuel prices and competition made fleets far more price sensitive it settled near 25% fleets / 75% truck stops — on a typical $2.00 transaction, $0.50 from the fleet and $1.50 from the truck stop.

Who Decides Whether the Interaction Happens?¶

  • Price sensitivity is one reason to go easy on a side. Here is another

  • Subsidize the side that chooses whether an interaction happens at all

    • The fleet decides which stops its drivers may use

    • The searcher decides which search engine to open

    • The cardholder decides which card to present

  • Lose that side and every participant on the other side loses volume

  • Our DBDSD_BD_SDB​DS​ captures the gate, but not who initiates each interaction

  • Platforms also choose what to price

    • Access fees charge for joining; usage fees charge per interaction

    • OpenTable: restaurants pay a monthly fee plus a per-seat fee; diners pay nothing and earn rewards

    • Amex: cardholders pay an annual fee, then earn rewards on every swipe, a negative usage price

  • When a platform charges to join, the other side’s size enters each member’s decision directly. We build that model next

Build 4: Pay to Join¶

  • So far, prices are per interaction. Many platforms instead charge to join: a dating-app subscription, a mall’s monthly rent, a nightclub cover charge

  • Now a buyer decides whether to join by asking how many sellers she gains access to, just as in Platforms and Matchmakers

  • Let nBn_BnB​ and nSn_SnS​ be the number of members on each side, and pBp_BpB​, pSp_SpS​ the membership fees

  • Each buyer values each seller at αB\alpha_BαB​; each seller values each buyer at αS\alpha_SαS​

  • A buyer’s net price is her fee minus the value of the sellers she reaches: pB−αBnSp_B-\alpha_Bn_SpB​−αB​nS​

  • Membership responds to the net price:

nB=aB−bB(pB−αBnS),nS=aS−bS(pS−αSnB).n_B=a_B-b_B(p_B-\alpha_Bn_S), \qquad n_S=a_S-b_S(p_S-\alpha_Sn_B).nB​=aB​−bB​(pB​−αB​nS​),nS​=aS​−bS​(pS​−αS​nB​).
(13)

  • This is a membership externality: the cross-side effect now sits inside each demand curve

Solving for Membership¶

  • nBn_BnB​ depends on nSn_SnS​, and nSn_SnS​ depends on nBn_BnB​. For given fees, we need membership that is consistent on both sides

  • This is the two-sided version of the fulfilled-expectations equilibrium from Platforms and Matchmakers

  • Both equations are linear in (nB,nS)(n_B,n_S)(nB​,nS​), so one linear solve finds it:

[1−bBαB−bSαS1][nBnS]=[aB−bBpBaS−bSpS].\begin{bmatrix}1 & -b_B\alpha_B\\ -b_S\alpha_S & 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}n_B\\ n_S\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}a_B-b_Bp_B\\ a_S-b_Sp_S\end{bmatrix}.[1−bS​αS​​−bB​αB​1​][nB​nS​​]=[aB​−bB​pB​aS​−bS​pS​​].
(14)

  • We need bBαB bSαS<1b_B\alpha_B\,b_S\alpha_S<1bB​αB​bS​αS​<1. Otherwise each new member attracts more than one new member on the way back, and membership never settles

  • With a cost fff per member, profit is

π(pB,pS)=(pB−f) nB+(pS−f) nS.\pi(p_B,p_S)=(p_B-f)\,n_B+(p_S-f)\,n_S.π(pB​,pS​)=(pB​−f)nB​+(pS​−f)nS​.
(15)
function members(p_B, p_S, m)
    A = [1.0 -m.bB * m.αB; -m.bS * m.αS 1.0]
    rhs = [m.aB - m.bB * p_B, m.aS - m.bS * p_S]
    return max.(A \ rhs, 0.0)
end

function join_profit(p_B, p_S, m)
    n_B, n_S = members(p_B, p_S, m)
    return (p_B - m.f) * n_B + (p_S - m.f) * n_S
end

Equal Sensitivity, Unequal Value¶

  • Both sides are equally price sensitive: bB=bS=1b_B=b_S=1bB​=bS​=1, equal intercepts, member cost f=2f=2f=2

Subsidize the Side the Other Side Values¶

  • Buyers pay about -0.30: the platform pays them to join, even though each costs f=2f=2f=2 to serve

  • Sellers pay about 12.30

  • Price sensitivity is identical, so this subsidy is not about which side is easier to lose

  • Each extra buyer is worth αS=1.2\alpha_S=1.2αS​=1.2 to every seller, and the platform collects that value through the seller fee

  • Armstrong (2006) derives the optimal fee in this model:

pB∗=f⏟cost+nBbB⏟one-sided markup−αSnS⏟value to all sellers.p_B^*=\underbrace{f}_{\text{cost}}+\underbrace{\frac{n_B}{b_B}}_{\text{one-sided markup}}-\underbrace{\alpha_Sn_S}_{\text{value to all sellers}}.pB∗​=costf​​+one-sided markupbB​nB​​​​−value to all sellersαS​nS​​​.
(16)

  • The last term is the discount: one more buyer creates αS\alpha_SαS​ for each of nSn_SnS​ sellers

  • Two models, two reasons to subsidize a side:

    • Per-interaction fees: subsidize the side that is easier to lose (large bbb)

    • Membership fees: subsidize the side the other side values most (large α\alphaα on the other side)

Pricing Can Help a Platform Ignite¶

  • Subsidizing one side can help solve the chicken-and-egg problem

  • Reaching critical mass strengthens cross-side participation and may trigger tipping

  • Same-side congestion or rivalry can push in the opposite direction

  • Our static linear model compresses those dynamics into demand curves

  • The lesson is not “always subsidize buyers”; it is “price the network effect, not just the transaction”

Platforms in Court¶

  • Ohio v. American Express treated credit-card transactions as a two-sided platform market

    • Evaluating merchant fees alone can miss cardholder rewards and changes in transaction volume

  • Epic Games v. Apple put platform market definition and rules between users and developers at center stage

    • A developer commission cannot be interpreted without asking how users and developers respond together

  • Courts increasingly require two-sided analysis when the relevant market links both groups

  • Two-sided analysis does not mean every platform practice is efficient or lawful

Multihoming and the Competitive Bottleneck¶

  • Multihoming means participating on more than one platform

  • Armstrong’s competitive-bottleneck insight in one line:

    • Platforms court the side that single-homes and tax the side that multihomes

  • the diagram below shows why: access to the single-homers is what rival platforms compete over

  • Winning a single-homing participant can give access to that participant across competing platforms

  • This adds competitive strategy to the cross-side logic in our monopoly model

Single-homing users are split between rival platforms and courted with low or negative prices, while multihoming users connect to both platforms and are taxed with higher prices, because access to the single-homers is what the platforms are really competing for.

Takeaways¶

  • A platform chooses a vector of prices, not one price

  • Cross-side network effects make participation on each side affect the value of the other

  • The price-sensitive side may optimally pay zero or receive a subsidy

  • When platforms charge to join, the side the other side values most may be subsidized instead

  • Price structure can change volume even when the total price level does not

  • Grid search, hand-derived first-order conditions, and Newton’s method tell the same story

  • As with Braess' paradox, familiar pieces can produce a surprising system-level result
