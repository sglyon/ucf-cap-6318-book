You Need a Kidney¶

  • Roughly 100,000 people are on the U.S. organ-transplant waitlist

  • Paying someone for a kidney is a felony in the United States, and organ sales are prohibited nearly everywhere

  • Yet thousands of transplants happen each year between people who began as strangers

    • An incompatible donor–patient pair can swap with another pair

    • An algorithm searches for compatible cycles and chains

  • Algorithms from the same matching family assign every U.S. medical resident to a hospital and many New York City students to public schools

  • Some markets are repugnant to clear with prices—or simply too complex—so we design the match

  • This marriage of theory and practice earned Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences

When a Price Is Not the Answer¶

  • Last time we separated a platform’s price structure from its price level

  • But what if the right price is illegal, unacceptable, or not enough to identify who should meet whom?

  • A hospital cares which doctor it hires; a doctor cares which hospital hires them

  • These are two-sided markets, but the central object is a pair—not a transaction price

  • Today we replace willingness-to-pay with preference rankings

Build 1: The Stable Matching Problem¶

  • Let DDD be the set of doctors and HHH the set of hospitals, with ∣D∣=∣H∣|D| = |H|∣D∣=∣H∣

  • Every doctor ranks every hospital; every hospital ranks every doctor

  • There are no prices in this model

  • Let μ:D→H\mu : D \rightarrow Hμ:D→H be a matching: an assignment (not a preference) that pairs each doctor with exactly one hospital, with no two doctors sharing a hospital

    • μ(d)\mu(d)μ(d) is the hospital assigned to doctor ddd

    • We often write μ\muμ as its set of pairs, e.g. {(d1,h1),(d2,h2)}\{(d_1,h_1),(d_2,h_2)\}{(d1​,h1​),(d2​,h2​)}

  • Let d≻hd′d \succ_h d'd≻h​d′ mean hospital hhh prefers doctor ddd to d′d'd′; likewise h≻dh′h \succ_d h'h≻d​h′ for doctors

  • A doctor–hospital pair (d,h)(d,h)(d,h) with μ(d)≠h\mu(d) \neq hμ(d)=h is a blocking pair when, letting d′=μ−1(h)d' = \mu^{-1}(h)d′=μ−1(h) be the doctor currently assigned to hhh:

    • h≻dμ(d)h \succ_d \mu(d)h≻d​μ(d): doctor ddd prefers hhh to its own assignment, and

    • d≻hd′d \succ_h d'd≻h​d′: hospital hhh prefers ddd to its current doctor

  • A matching is stable when it has no blocking pair

Stability Is an Equilibrium Concept¶

  • Recall Nash equilibrium from week 8: no player can profitably deviate on their own

  • Stability asks a closely related question: can two agents profitably deviate together?

  • The deviations here are pairwise

  • If a blocking pair exists, the announced matching is fragile

    • The doctor and hospital would rather abandon it and match with each other

  • Stable does not mean fair, unique, or socially optimal

  • It means the matching can defend itself against every pairwise objection

Fixing the Match by Hand¶

  • Swap the first two assignments:

    • μ1={(d1,h2),(d2,h1),(d3,h3)}\mu_1 = \{(d_1,h_2),(d_2,h_1),(d_3,h_3)\}μ1​={(d1​,h2​),(d2​,h1​),(d3​,h3​)}

  • d2d_2d2​ now has their first choice

  • d1d_1d1​ wants h1h_1h1​, but h1h_1h1​ prefers its current doctor d2d_2d2​

  • d3d_3d3​ wants h2h_2h2​ or h1h_1h1​, but both hospitals prefer their current doctors

  • No doctor–hospital pair wants to leave together

  • Therefore μ1\mu_1μ1​ is stable

  • Checking every pair works for 3×33 \times 33×3; we need an algorithm for 50×5050 \times 5050×50

Build 2: Deferred Acceptance¶

  • Gale and Shapley’s deferred-acceptance algorithm proceeds in rounds

    1. Each unmatched proposer proposes to their best choice that has not rejected them

    2. Each receiver tentatively holds its best offer so far and rejects the rest

    3. Rejected proposers try their next choice

    4. Repeat until no proposer is unmatched

  • Tentative is doing important work: a receiver may trade up later

  • The process must stop because no proposer visits the same receiver twice

  • The remarkable result: it always stops at a stable matching

  • this figure traces doctor-proposing deferred acceptance on the tiny market above, round by round

Doctor-proposing deferred acceptance on the tiny market, traced round by round: proposals as arrows, rejections as red X marks, and tentative holds as bold green edges. Round 4 reaches μ1={(d1,h2),(d2,h1),(d3,h3)}\mu_1 = \{(d_1,h_2),(d_2,h_1),(d_3,h_3)\}μ1​={(d1​,h2​),(d2​,h1​),(d3​,h3​)}, the stable matching found by hand.

Rankings as Matrices¶

  • We store each side’s preferences in two matrices that answer opposite questions

MatrixIndexed byValue
doctor_prefs[i, k]doctor iii, choice number kkkthe hospital that is doctor iii’s kkk-th choice
doctor_rank[i, j]doctor iii, hospital jjjthe position of hjh_jhj​ on doctor iii’s list (1 = first choice)

  • Example: d3d_3d3​’s list is h2≻h1≻h3h_2 \succ h_1 \succ h_3h2​≻h1​≻h3​

    • doctor_prefs[3, 1] = 2: d3d_3d3​’s first choice is h2h_2h2​

    • doctor_rank[3, 2] = 1: h2h_2h2​ is first on d3d_3d3​’s list

  • Each row of the rank matrix is the inverse permutation of the matching row of the preference matrix

  • In this tiny market the two matrices happen to be equal, because every row only swaps options and a swap is its own inverse

    • A row like [2, 3, 1] shows the difference: its rank row is [3, 1, 2]

  • We keep both because each makes a different operation simple

    • Proposing walks down a preference list: doctor_prefs[d, k]

    • Checking stability compares two options in one step: ddd prefers hhh to μ(d)\mu(d)μ(d) exactly when doctor_rank[d, h] < doctor_rank[d, μ(d)]

"""
    preference_ranks(preferences)

Convert ranked lists into rank lookups.

Row `p` of `preferences` lists person `p`'s options from best to worst:
`preferences[p, k]` is person `p`'s `k`-th choice.
Row `p` of the result answers the reverse question:
`ranks[p, o]` is the position of option `o` on person `p`'s list (1 = first choice).

Each row of the result is the inverse permutation of the matching row of the input.
The lookup lets deferred acceptance compare two candidates with one comparison:
hospital `h` prefers doctor `d` to `d′` exactly when `ranks[h, d] < ranks[h, d′]`.

# Example
A row `[2, 3, 1]` ("2 first, then 3, then 1") becomes `[3, 1, 2]`
(option 1 is ranked 3rd, option 2 is 1st, option 3 is 2nd).
A row that only swaps options, such as `[2, 1, 3]`, is its own inverse,
so the input and output rows look identical.
"""
function preference_ranks(preferences)
    n_people, n_options = size(preferences)
    ranks = zeros(Int, n_people, n_options)
    for person in 1:n_people
        for (rank, option) in enumerate(preferences[person, :])
            ranks[person, option] = rank
        end
    end
    return ranks
end

doctor_rank = preference_ranks(doctor_prefs)
hospital_rank = preference_ranks(hospital_prefs)

Computing a Stable Matching¶

  • held_by_receiver[h] records the proposer tentatively held by receiver hhh

  • next_choice[d] records where proposer ddd should apply next

  • Our queue contains proposers who are currently unmatched

  • Rejection sends a proposer to the back of that queue

"""
    deferred_acceptance(proposer_prefs, receiver_rank)

Run Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance with one side proposing, and return a stable matching.

- `proposer_prefs[p, k]` is proposer `p`'s `k`-th choice of receiver (see `preference_ranks`)
- `receiver_rank[r, p]` is the position of proposer `p` on receiver `r`'s list (1 = first choice)

Each unmatched proposer applies to the next receiver on their list. A receiver tentatively
holds the best proposal so far and rejects the other; a rejected proposer rejoins the queue.
Both sides must have the same size.

Returns `matching`, where `matching[p]` is the receiver assigned to proposer `p`.
Swap the arguments' roles to let the other side propose.
"""
function deferred_acceptance(proposer_prefs, receiver_rank)
    n_proposers, n_receivers = size(proposer_prefs)
    n_proposers == n_receivers || error("This version expects equal sides")
    size(receiver_rank) == (n_receivers, n_proposers) || error("Rank dimensions do not match")

    # Setup: everyone starts unmatched; each proposer will begin with their first choice
    next_choice = ones(Int, n_proposers)
    held_by_receiver = zeros(Int, n_receivers)
    proposal_queue = collect(1:n_proposers)
    queue_head = 1

    # Step 4: repeat until no proposer is unmatched (the queue of unmatched proposers is empty).
    # We handle one proposal at a time instead of a whole round at once;
    # deferred acceptance reaches the same matching in either order.
    while queue_head <= length(proposal_queue)
        # Step 1: the next unmatched proposer proposes to their best choice
        # that has not rejected them yet
        proposer = proposal_queue[queue_head]
        queue_head += 1
        next_choice[proposer] <= n_receivers || error("A proposer exhausted all choices")

        receiver = proposer_prefs[proposer, next_choice[proposer]]
        next_choice[proposer] += 1
        incumbent = held_by_receiver[receiver]

        # Step 2: the receiver tentatively holds its best offer so far and rejects the other
        # Step 3: a rejected proposer rejoins the queue; `next_choice` already points to
        # their next choice, so they will try it on their next turn
        if incumbent == 0
            held_by_receiver[receiver] = proposer          # first offer: hold it
        elseif receiver_rank[receiver, proposer] < receiver_rank[receiver, incumbent]
            held_by_receiver[receiver] = proposer          # trade up: hold the new offer...
            push!(proposal_queue, incumbent)               # ...and reject the incumbent (Step 3)
        else
            push!(proposal_queue, proposer)                # keep the incumbent; reject the proposer (Step 3)
        end
    end

    # Convert who-holds-whom into each proposer's assigned receiver
    matching = zeros(Int, n_proposers)
    for receiver in 1:n_receivers
        matching[held_by_receiver[receiver]] = receiver
    end
    return matching
end

"""
    is_stable(matching, proposer_rank, receiver_rank)

Return `true` when `matching` is a perfect matching with no blocking pair.

- `matching[p]` is the receiver assigned to proposer `p`
- `proposer_rank[p, r]` and `receiver_rank[r, p]` are rank lookups (1 = best), as returned by `preference_ranks`

Checks every pair `(p, r)`: the pair blocks when `p` ranks `r` above `matching[p]` **and**
`r` ranks `p` above its current partner. Returns `false` if `matching` is not one-to-one.
"""
function is_stable(matching, proposer_rank, receiver_rank)
    n = length(matching)
    sort(matching) == collect(1:n) || return false

    receiver_partner = zeros(Int, n)
    for proposer in 1:n
        receiver_partner[matching[proposer]] = proposer
    end

    for proposer in 1:n, receiver in 1:n
        current_receiver = matching[proposer]
        current_proposer = receiver_partner[receiver]
        proposer_wants = proposer_rank[proposer, receiver] < proposer_rank[proposer, current_receiver]
        receiver_wants = receiver_rank[receiver, proposer] < receiver_rank[receiver, current_proposer]
        proposer_wants && receiver_wants && return false
    end
    return true
end

Algorithm Design Is Market Power¶

  • Both matchings are stable—but they distribute rank very differently

  • Doctor-proposing deferred acceptance gives every doctor their best outcome among all stable matchings

  • Hospital-proposing deferred acceptance gives every hospital its best stable outcome

  • This is proposer-optimality

  • this figure is the visual answer to Exercise 1’s prediction: which side gets its first choice?

  • The NRMP redesigned its algorithm in 1997 to be applicant-proposing precisely because the choice of proposer matters

  • Deferred acceptance is also strategy-proof for proposers: truthful rankings are a dominant strategy for that side

  • Recall second-price auctions from week 9: a different mechanism, but the same truthfulness flavor

  • A neutral-looking line of algorithm design can shift market power

In the 2×22\times 22×2 market from Exercise 1, doctor-proposing deferred acceptance finds the diagonal matching, giving every doctor its first choice; hospital-proposing deferred acceptance finds the cross matching, giving every hospital its first choice. Both matchings are stable.

Hospitals Have Capacity¶

  • The real NRMP is many-to-one: a hospital can accept qh>1q_h > 1qh​>1 residents

  • We extend deferred acceptance so each hospital tentatively holds up to its capacity

  • When a hospital is full, it rejects its least-preferred held doctor

  • When total capacity exceeds the number of doctors, some seats stay empty; when it falls short, some doctors stay unmatched

  • Algorithm (doctor-proposing; hospital hhh holds at most qhq_hqh​ doctors):

    1. Every doctor starts unmatched, pointing at their first choice

    2. Pick an unmatched doctor who still has hospitals left; they propose to the next hospital on their list

    3. The hospital adds the doctor to the set it holds

    4. If that set now exceeds qhq_hqh​, the hospital rejects its least-preferred held doctor, who becomes unmatched

    5. Repeat steps 2-4 until no unmatched doctor has a hospital left to try

  • Compared with deferred_acceptance: a hospital holds a set instead of one doctor, and a doctor can run out of hospitals

  • We return one hospital per doctor, or 0 for a doctor left unmatched

  • The checks below use two markets: the tiny market with every capacity set to 1, and a four-doctor market with only three seats

"""
    deferred_acceptance_capacities(proposer_prefs, receiver_rank, capacities)

Doctor-proposing deferred acceptance where hospital `h` holds up to `capacities[h]` doctors.

- `proposer_prefs[d, k]` is doctor `d`'s `k`-th choice of hospital
- `receiver_rank[h, d]` is the position of doctor `d` on hospital `h`'s list (1 = first choice)

Returns a `Vector{Int}` with one hospital per doctor, or `0` for a doctor left unmatched.
With every capacity equal to 1 and equal sides, this matches `deferred_acceptance`.
"""
function deferred_acceptance_capacities(proposer_prefs, receiver_rank, capacities)
    n_proposers, n_receivers = size(proposer_prefs)
    size(receiver_rank) == (n_receivers, n_proposers) || error("Rank dimensions do not match")
    length(capacities) == n_receivers || error("Need one capacity per hospital")

    # Step 1: every doctor starts unmatched, pointing at their first choice
    next_choice = ones(Int, n_proposers)
    held = [Int[] for _ in 1:n_receivers]   # held[h]: the doctors hospital h tentatively holds
    proposal_queue = collect(1:n_proposers)
    queue_head = 1

    # Step 5: repeat until no unmatched doctor has a hospital left to try
    while queue_head <= length(proposal_queue)
        # Step 2: an unmatched doctor proposes to the next hospital on their list
        proposer = proposal_queue[queue_head]
        queue_head += 1
        next_choice[proposer] <= n_receivers || continue   # no hospitals left: stays unmatched
        receiver = proposer_prefs[proposer, next_choice[proposer]]
        next_choice[proposer] += 1

        # Step 3: the hospital adds the doctor to the set it holds
        push!(held[receiver], proposer)

        # Step 4: over capacity, so reject the least-preferred held doctor, who becomes unmatched
        if length(held[receiver]) > capacities[receiver]
            worst = argmax(d -> receiver_rank[receiver, d], held[receiver])
            filter!(!=(worst), held[receiver])
            push!(proposal_queue, worst)
        end
    end

    # Convert each hospital's held set into each doctor's hospital (0 = unmatched)
    matching = zeros(Int, n_proposers)
    for receiver in 1:n_receivers, proposer in held[receiver]
        matching[proposer] = receiver
    end
    return matching
end
# Check the implementation on two markets
function test_capacities(f)
    got = f(doctor_prefs, hospital_rank, [1, 1, 1])
    want = deferred_acceptance(doctor_prefs, hospital_rank)
    got == want || error("Capacities [1, 1, 1] should match deferred_acceptance: expected $want, got $got")

    # Four doctors, two hospitals; h1 holds two doctors, h2 holds one
    prefs = [1 2;   # d1: h1 ≻ h2
             1 2;   # d2: h1 ≻ h2

Values, Payoffs, and Preferred Sellers¶

  • We use E&K’s example from Figures 10.5–10.6

  • Let I={x,y,z}I = \{x, y, z\}I={x,y,z} be the set of buyers and J={a,b,c}J = \{a, b, c\}J={a,b,c} the set of sellers, each selling one house

  • Let VijV_{ij}Vij​ be buyer iii’s value for seller jjj’s house and pjp_jpj​ be that house’s price

    • In code, row iii of the value matrix is buyer iii and column jjj is seller jjj

  • Buyer iii’s payoff from buying house jjj is Vij−pjV_{ij} - p_jVij​−pj​; not buying gives zero. Sellers value keeping their houses at zero

  • Let the preferred-seller graph link each buyer iii to every seller jjj that maximizes Vij−pjV_{ij} - p_jVij​−pj​, provided that payoff is nonnegative; ties keep every maximizer

  • A perfect matching uses these edges to pair every buyer and seller exactly once

house_values = [12 4 2; 8 7 6; 7 5 2]

"""
    preferred_sellers(values, prices)

Build the preferred-seller graph at the given prices.

`values[i, j]` is buyer `i`'s valuation of seller `j`'s house and `prices[j]` is seller `j`'s price.
Returns a `Bool` matrix whose entry `[i, j]` is `true` when seller `j` gives buyer `i` the
highest payoff `values[i, j] - prices[j]` and that payoff is nonnegative.
"""
function preferred_sellers(values, prices)
    payoffs = values .- permutedims(prices)
    return (payoffs .== maximum(payoffs; dims=2)) .& (payoffs .>= 0)
end

Too Many Buyers, Too Few Houses¶

  • At zero prices, all three buyers point only to seller aaa

  • Let T⊆IT \subseteq IT⊆I be a group of buyers and N(T)⊆JN(T) \subseteq JN(T)⊆J the sellers any of them prefers

  • TTT is a constricted set when ∣N(T)∣<∣T∣|N(T)| < |T|∣N(T)∣<∣T∣: fewer preferred sellers than buyers

  • Hall’s matching theorem: with equal sides, a perfect matching exists exactly when every group of buyers has at least as many neighboring sellers as buyers

  • Prices are market-clearing when the preferred-seller graph has a perfect matching; ties may still require coordination

  • For our tiny markets, we can enumerate assignments and buyer subsets. This is transparent, but grows too quickly for large markets

"""
    seller_orders(sellers)

All orderings (permutations) of `sellers`. Each ordering `m` is a candidate one-to-one
assignment in which buyer `i` gets seller `m[i]`. There are `n!` orderings, so use this only
for tiny markets.
"""
function seller_orders(sellers)
    isempty(sellers) && return [Int[]]
    return [vcat(seller, rest) for seller in sellers
            for rest in seller_orders(filter(!=(seller), sellers))]
end

"""
    constricted_buyers(preferred)

Find a constricted set in the preferred-seller graph `preferred` (from `preferred_sellers`).

Searches buyer subsets `T` by brute force and returns the first `(buyers, neighbors)` with
fewer preferred sellers `N(T)` than buyers. Errors if none exists, which by Hall's theorem
means a perfect matching of preferred sellers exists.
"""
function constricted_buyers(preferred)
    n = size(preferred, 1)
    for mask in 1:(2^n - 1)
        buyers = [i for i in 1:n if (mask & (1 << (i - 1))) != 0]
        neighbors = findall(vec(any(preferred[buyers, :]; dims=1)))
        length(neighbors) < length(buyers) && return buyers, neighbors
    end
    error("No constricted set: a perfect matching must exist")
end

An Ascending-Price Auction¶

  • Algorithm (E&K §10.4):

    1. Set every price to zero

    2. Build the preferred-seller graph at the current prices

    3. If it has a perfect matching, stop: these prices clear the market

    4. Otherwise find a constricted set of buyers and raise the price of every seller in its neighborhood by one

    5. Let pmin⁡=min⁡j∈Jpjp_{\min} = \min_{j \in J} p_jpmin​=minj∈J​pj​ be the lowest price across all sellers. Subtract pmin⁡p_{\min}pmin​ from every price so the cheapest house costs 0 again, then return to step 2

  • Step 5 lowers every price by the same amount, so each buyer’s payoffs Vij−pjV_{ij} - p_jVij​−pj​ all rise by pmin⁡p_{\min}pmin​ and their ranking of sellers does not change. It keeps prices from drifting upward and leaves a free house, so every buyer has a nonnegative option

  • The diagram below traces all four rounds: each round’s constricted set outlined in red, the final matching in green

"""
    clearing_auction(values)

Run the ascending-price auction (E&K §10.4) on a tiny square market and return
market-clearing prices.

`values[i, j]` is buyer `i`'s integer valuation of seller `j`'s house. Each round raises by 1
the price of every seller in a constricted set's neighborhood, then shifts prices so the
lowest is 0. Stops when some one-to-one assignment uses only preferred sellers.

Returns a named tuple:
- `prices`: market-clearing prices, one per seller
- `matching`: `matching[i]` is the seller assigned to buyer `i`
- `history`: the price vector at the start of each round
"""
function clearing_auction(values)
    n = size(values, 1)
    @assert size(values, 2) == n && 1 <= n <= 8 "Use a tiny square market"
    @assert eltype(values) <: Integer && all(values .>= 0)
    orders = seller_orders(collect(1:n))
    prices = zeros(Int, n)
    history = Vector{Int}[]
    while true
        push!(history, copy(prices))
        preferred = preferred_sellers(values, prices)
        for matching in orders
            if all(preferred[i, matching[i]] for i in 1:n)
                return (; prices, matching, history)
            end
        end
        buyers, neighbors = constricted_buyers(preferred)
        prices[neighbors] .+= 1
        prices .-= minimum(prices)
    end
end

Private Choices, Maximum Total Value¶

  • Prices move through [0,0,0], [1,0,0], [2,0,0], and [3,1,0]

  • At the final prices, assign xxx to aaa, yyy to ccc, and zzz to bbb: total value is 12+6+5=2312+6+5=2312+6+5=23

  • Under these assumptions, market-clearing prices always exist, and every perfect matching of preferred sellers maximizes total valuation (E&K §10.3)

  • Why optimal? Each buyer maximizes value minus price. Summing across buyers, every complete assignment subtracts the same sum of house prices

  • Prices decide who keeps the surplus, not how much there is

    • Each price is paid by a buyer and received by a seller, so it cancels in the sum of everyone’s payoffs

    • Total welfare equals total valuation: 23 here

    • At prices (3,1,0)(3, 1, 0)(3,1,0), sellers receive 3+0+1=43 + 0 + 1 = 43+0+1=4 and buyers keep 23−4=1923 - 4 = 1923−4=19

  • Deferred acceptance guarantees stability with ordinal rankings and favors proposers. Here monetary valuations let us compare total surplus; maximizing it does not settle who gets that surplus

  • Airbnb combines matching and prices. Next we will inspect what that combination looks like in data

When Reviews Keep a Market Alive¶

  • A guest discovers a listing’s true quality only after staying

  • Guests pay expected quality; good hosts may reject that price and leave

  • Average quality falls, bids fall, and more good hosts leave

  • This adverse selection changes who participates: the lemons mechanism (E&K §22.6)

  • Could trade shrink even if guests value every listing above its host’s cost?

  • The diagram below draws the spiral, and the circuit breaker that reputation adds

Without reputation, guests pay every listing the market’s average quality; hosts whose costs exceed that price leave, pulling the average down and repeating the spiral. Reputation prices each host on their own track record instead of the average, breaking the loop so good hosts keep earning above cost.

A Tiny Market with Hidden Quality¶

  • Draw fixed host qualities qiq_iqi​ uniformly from [0,1][0,1][0,1]; guests value service at qiq_iqi​, while hosts require 0.8qi0.8q_i0.8qi​

  • Guests pay mean remaining quality; hosts requiring more exit permanently

  • Each host serves one guest per round; guests are plentiful and risk neutral

  • With reputation, guests instead estimate each host’s quality from their mean past rating (E&K §22.9)

  • Ratings are qiq_iqi​ plus independent noise. Assume five platform-funded trial transactions establish each host’s initial record, then every stay adds a rating

  • Mean ratings estimate quality; they are not exact Bayesian expectations. Trials require funding

"""
    quality_market(quality; reputation=false, noise=0.12, rounds=30, seed=2209)

Simulate a market with hidden host quality, with or without a reputation system.

`quality[i]` is host `i`'s true quality; host `i` stays only while the price they receive is
at least `0.8 * quality[i]`, and exits permanently otherwise.
- `reputation=false`: every host receives the average quality of active hosts (pooling)
- `reputation=true`: each host receives their average rating, a noisy measure of their quality
  (noise standard deviation `noise`); only active hosts earn new ratings

Returns a `DataFrame` with one row per round: `share` of hosts still active, average
`quality` of active hosts, and `high_share` of high-quality (≥ 0.75) hosts still active.
"""
function quality_market(quality; reputation=false, noise=0.12, rounds=30, seed=2209)
    rng = MersenneTwister(seed)
    n_hosts = length(quality)
    active = trues(n_hosts)
    reservation = 0.8 .* quality

    counts = fill(5, n_hosts)
    rating_sum = 5 .* quality .+ noise .* vec(sum(randn(rng, n_hosts, 5); dims=2))

    high_quality = quality .>= 0.75
    history = DataFrame(round=Int[], share=Float64[], quality=Float64[], high_share=Float64[])

    for round in 0:rounds
        average_quality = any(active) ? mean(quality[active]) : NaN
        high_share = any(high_quality) ? mean(active[high_quality]) : NaN
        push!(history, (round, mean(active), average_quality, high_share))

        (round == rounds || !any(active)) && break

        prices = reputation ? clamp.(rating_sum ./ counts, 0, 1) : fill(average_quality, n_hosts)
        active .&= prices .>= reservation

        if reputation
            # Only completed transactions produce new ratings.
            rating_sum[active] .+= quality[active] .+ noise .* randn(rng, count(active))
            counts[active] .+= 1
        end
    end

    return history
end

Takeaways: The Platforms Unit¶

  • Platforms and Matchmakers: Platforms create value by connecting sides through same-side / cross-side network effects

  • Ignition: They solve a chicken-and-egg problem, fight toward critical mass, and may experience tipping

  • Platform Pricing: They choose price structure as well as price level, often subsidizing one side

  • Today: They sometimes replace prices with matching algorithms entirely

  • Market-clearing prices: Prices can also support a matching that maximizes total valuation

  • Reputation: Information about quality can keep good sellers participating; governance helps make that information credible

  • Multihoming can soften lock-in, but it does not remove the platform’s power to rank, recommend, price, or match

  • Stable matching shows that the rules connecting agents determine who benefits—even when no money changes hands

  • Matchmaking is the oldest business model—and maybe the newest: GPU and model marketplaces are two-sided markets for intelligence itself
